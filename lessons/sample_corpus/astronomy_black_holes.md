# Black Holes

Regions of spacetime where gravity is strong enough that nothing, not even
light, can escape once it crosses a boundary.

## The event horizon

The event horizon is the boundary itself. It is not a physical surface — there
is nothing there to touch. It is simply the point of no return, defined by how
fast you would have to travel to escape, which exceeds the speed of light.

You would notice nothing unusual crossing it. No drama, no visible edge. Only
afterward would you be unable to send anything back.

## How they form

When a massive star exhausts its fuel, outward pressure stops. The core collapses
under its own gravity. If the remaining mass exceeds a threshold, nothing else
can support it, and it becomes a black hole.

Not every collapsing star makes one. Below a certain mass the remnant becomes a
neutron star or a white dwarf instead.

## Why gravity bends light

Mass curves spacetime. Gravity is not a force pulling objects together — it is
the shape of spacetime itself. Light follows that shape, which is why it
appears to bend. A sufficiently concentrated mass curves spacetime so sharply
that every possible path leads inward.

## Observation

Astronomers do not observe black holes directly. They infer them from the
motion of nearby stars, from accretion disks heating to millions of degrees, and
from gravitational effects on light passing nearby. The first direct image came
from a telescope observing the shadow of the black hole at the centre of a
galaxy.
